China's Chang'e 5 and Chang'e 6 brought lunar soil to Earth from both sides of the Moon: the first from the near side, the second from the far side.

## Chang'e 5: Oceanus Procellarum, 2020

According to the team of the American LRO camera, Chang'e 5 landed on December 1, 2020 at 15:11 UTC on the basaltic plain of Oceanus Procellarum (Ocean of Storms). The next day LRO flew over the landing site and imaged the vehicle in the middle of a triangle of craters. The team calculated the coordinates: 43.0576° N, 308.0839° E (that is, 51.92° W), elevation −2,570 m, accurate to ±20 m.

[[figure:ce5]]

In the LROC team's assessment, the local plain is a flow of very fluid basaltic lava that poured out somewhere between one and two billion years ago. The sample was brought back precisely to determine its age and composition accurately. According to a paper in Nature Communications, Chang'e 5 delivered 1,731 g of regolith to Earth. The study of these samples, published in the journal Nature, is titled "Two-billion-year-old volcanism on the Moon from Chang'e-5 basalts".

[[figure:ce5-region]]

## Chang'e 6: the first from the far side, 2024

Chang'e 6 landed on June 1, 2024 on the far side of the Moon, on the southern edge of the Apollo basin, which is 492 km across. On June 7 LRO imaged it on the edge of a degraded crater about 50 m across. The LROC team determined the coordinates: 41.6385° S, 206.0148° E, 5,256 m below the mean surface level, accurate to ±30 m. The soil around the vehicle became brighter: it was blown away by the jet of the landing engine, as around other landers.

[[figure:ce6]]

According to the LROC team, the landing site lies on a basalt flow that poured out south of Chaffee S crater about 3.1 billion years ago and flowed eastward. This flow differs from the neighbouring, slightly older one (about 3.3 billion years) in its higher content of iron and titanium oxides.

[[figure:ce6-region]]

According to the paper in Nature Communications, the Chang'e 6 mission collected 1,935.3 g of samples in the South Pole–Aitken basin in two ways, with a scoop and with a drill; the paper gives the date June 25, 2024. The study of these basalts in the journal Nature is titled "Lunar farside volcanism 2.8 billion years ago from Chang'e-6 basalts".
