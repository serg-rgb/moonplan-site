On November 17, 1970, the Soviet Luna 17 station landed in Mare Imbrium (Sea of Rains). After 2 hours 41 minutes, an eight-wheeled vehicle weighing 756 kg, Lunokhod 1, drove down the ramp onto the soil. It was expected to work for three lunar days. It worked for eleven.

## How the rover was built

According to NASA, Lunokhod 1 was about 1.35 m tall and 2.15 m wide. Each of its eight wheels was driven separately, the vehicle had two forward speeds and two reverse, and the maximum was about 100 metres an hour. The scientific instruments were powered by solar panels mounted on the inside of a hinged lid and by chemical batteries.

On board were television cameras, the RIFMA X-ray fluorescence spectrometer for analysing the chemical composition of the soil, a penetrometer (a cone pressed into the soil to measure its strength), an X-ray telescope, a cosmic ray detector and a laser corner reflector of French manufacture.

The landing site turned out to be about 2,500 km from the area where Luna 16 had landed shortly before. The descent stage had two ramps, on opposite sides, so that the rover could drive off in whichever direction was more convenient. It drove down the eastern one.

## How it was driven

The rover was driven from Earth by a team of five drivers. According to NASA, they had to allow for a delay of about 5 seconds: a command went out, and the result appeared on the screen later. The LROC image shows that the rover was first sent south, made a loop across the plain and returned to the descent stage, and was then driven on to the north.

## What it achieved in 11 lunar days

In 322 Earth days of operation, Lunokhod 1 drove 10.54 km, sent to Earth more than 20,000 television images and 206 high-resolution panoramas, determined the chemical composition of the soil 25 times with its spectrometer and tested the strength of the soil with the penetrometer at 500 points.

At night the rover stood still: the lunar night lasts about two weeks, and the solar panels do not work during that time. After the night that began on September 14, 1971, contact could not be re-established. On October 4 the attempts were ended.

## Where it is now

During the mission the rover's reflector was tracked by laser only briefly and was then lost. The exact location of its final resting place was found from images by the team of the camera on the American Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter. After that, the Apache Point Observatory in the United States received a return signal from the laser reflector of Lunokhod 1. According to the LROC team, the reflector is valuable for studying the Moon because it stands far from the Apollo and Lunokhod 2 reflectors.

## What the Moon looked like from Earth that day

We calculated the phase for the moment of the Luna 17 landing, November 17, 1970 at 03:47 UTC. From Earth the Moon was waning and 84% lit: the full moon had already passed. The distance from the centre of the Earth to the centre of the Moon was 391,722 km.
