The second Soviet rover worked for a shorter time than the first, but drove more than three times as far. In four and a half months of 1973, Lunokhod 2 drove about 37 km across the floor of Le Monnier crater and the hills on its edge. According to the LRO camera team, lunar dust is what stopped it.

## Where it landed

The Luna 21 station with the rover was launched on January 11, 1973 and landed on the floor of Le Monnier crater on January 15. This is a crater 61 km across on the eastern edge of Mare Serenitatis (Sea of Serenity): its floor was once flooded by the same lavas that formed the mare itself. According to the LROC team, the coordinates of the landing site are 26.005° N, 30.406° E.

[[figure:region]]

The descent stage weighed 1,900 kg. The ramps for the rover to drive off were deployed on two sides, northwest and southeast. The rover drove down to the northwest, went around the stage, photographed it from several sides and set off to the south.

[[figure:lander]]

## How Lunokhod 2 was built

According to LROC, it was about 170 cm long, 160 cm wide and 135 cm tall, nearly round when seen from above, and ran on eight wheels. It could travel at 1 or 2 km per hour. It was driven from Earth by a team of Soviet operators.

The lid on top did two jobs. In the daytime it was opened, and the solar panels on its inner side supplied electricity. For the night the lid was closed, and the rover was kept warm by a fluid heated by the decay of polonium-210.

On board were three television cameras for driving, four panoramic cameras, a penetrometer for testing the soil, an instrument for observing the Sun's X-ray emission, a magnetometer, a radiometer and a laser corner reflector.

## The route

At first the rover headed south, toward the hills on the southern edge of the crater. The slopes proved difficult for it, and it was turned back onto the level floor of Le Monnier. Then it moved east and reached the Fossa Recta (Straight Rille): a narrow trough running from south-southwest to north-northeast. The rover went down its wall, crossed the floor, climbed out on the eastern side and went north, to the place of its final stop.

## Why it stopped

According to the LROC team, on May 9 the rover brushed the wall of a crater with its lid, and fine lunar dust stuck to it. When the lid was closed, the dust fell onto the radiators. The next time the lid was opened, the vehicle overheated and failed. The mission was officially ended on June 4, 1973.

[[figure:rover]]

The rover still stands where it stopped, facing southeast with its lid open. The LRO camera photographed it from an altitude of only 24 km, so the resolution of the image is about twice the usual.

## What the Moon looked like from Earth on landing day

We calculated the phase for January 15, 1973 at 23:35 UTC (the landing time according to NSSDCA). From Earth the Moon was waxing and 88% lit: 11.33 days had passed since the new moon on January 4. The distance from the centre of the Earth to the centre of the Moon was 363,044 km.
