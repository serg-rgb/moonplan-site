Planting by the Moon is one of the oldest habits in the vegetable garden. Grandmothers remember it, gardening calendars print it, and the arguments about it never die down. Below: what the tradition says, what science knows about it, and what to look at when you pick a planting day.

## What the tradition says

The basic rule is simple. Plants whose harvest grows above ground are sown on the waxing Moon, from new Moon to full Moon. Those whose harvest is in the ground go in on the waning Moon, from full Moon to new Moon. That is how the American Old Farmer's Almanac states it: as the moonlight dwindles night after night, it says, plants "head down into roots, tubers and bulbs". It counts carrots, onions and potatoes among the underground crops.

Terry Kelley, a gardening consultant at the University of Georgia, gave similar advice in 2005: plant above-ground crops "in the light of the Moon" and underground ones in its third quarter. By the same tradition, soil is prepared on the waxing Moon and loosened on the waning one. The author himself calls this an ancient theory and invites readers to test it in their own gardens.

Russian village tradition also tied sowing to the Moon, though less neatly. Dal's collection has "Sowing on the young Moon means a good harvest" right beside "Sow at the new Moon and the worms will eat it". More on that in our article on folk signs.

## What science says

In 2020 the journal Agronomy published a review by the Spanish researchers Olga Mayoral, Jordi Solbes, José Cantó and Tatiana Pina. They went through textbooks and monographs on agronomy, botany, horticulture and plant physiology, as well as scientific papers on the Moon's influence. Their conclusion is stated plainly: there is no reliable scientific evidence linking lunar phases to plant physiology, and the common farming practices tied to the phases have no scientific support.

The University of Illinois Extension's garden outreach service, retelling this review, writes that no consistent link has been found that would confirm any influence of the Moon on plants. Linda Chalker-Scott of Washington State University reached the same view in her long-standing article «Lunar control? Or lunacy?»: lunar cycles have almost no direct bearing on plants. The only indirect effect she allows concerns insects that feed in moonlight.

If you enjoy planting by the Moon, nothing stops you. Just make sure the day also suits the weather.

## What really sets sowing time

Agronomists name two main factors. The Purdue University vegetable calendar (USA) says the right time is set by the soil temperature the seeds need to germinate and by how much cold the plants themselves can take. For warm-season crops such as beans, tomatoes, squash and sweet corn it advises sowing only once the soil has warmed to at least 60 °F, about 16 °C. Cold-hardy vegetables, by the same calendar, go in 4 to 6 weeks before the last spring frost.

Dates of the last frost differ from place to place, and local weather services keep them. So a sensible scheme is this: look at the weather and the soil first, and if several days are suitable, pick the one that matches the phase of the Moon you want.
