More than a year and a half passed after the Apollo 1 fire before people got into an Apollo spacecraft again. Apollo 7 had to prove that the redesigned spacecraft was reliable. It flew only around Earth and without a lunar module: the task was to test the command and service module itself.

## The crew

Commander Walter Schirra, command module pilot Donn Eisele and lunar module pilot Walter Cunningham. All three had been the backups of the Apollo 1 crew.

[[figure:crew]]

## The launch

The Saturn IB rocket lifted off on October 11, 1968 at 15:02:45 UTC from Launch Complex 34, the very one where the Apollo 1 fire happened. The spacecraft entered an orbit of 228 × 282 km, which was later raised to 232 × 309 km. The mass of the command and service module with propellant was 14,781 kg.

[[figure:launch]]

## Flight timeline

| Date and time, UTC | Event |
|---|---|
| October 11, 15:02:45 | Launch from Complex 34 |
| October 11, ~3 h after launch | Rendezvous practice with the S-IVB third stage |
| October 14 | The first of seven TV broadcasts from on board |
| October 22, 11:11:48 | Splashdown in the Atlantic Ocean |

From launch to splashdown, 260 hours 9 minutes 3 seconds passed, that is 10 days 20 hours 9 minutes. In that time the spacecraft made 163 orbits.

[[figure:s-ivb]]

## Television from orbit

Seven TV broadcasts were made from Apollo 7. According to NASA, these were the first live TV transmissions from an American crewed spacecraft. The camera weighed about 2 kg (4.5 pounds). The first broadcast was planned for the second day but was postponed, and it took place on October 14, the fourth day of the flight. In 1969 the crew received a special Emmy Award for these broadcasts from the National Academy of Television Arts and Sciences.

## A cold in orbit

Soon after launch Schirra reported that he had a bad head cold. According to NASA, the astronauts did not wear their helmets during the landing.

[[figure:schirra]]

## The return

The command module splashed down on October 22, 1968 at 11:11:48 UTC in the Atlantic Ocean, about 200 nautical miles south-southwest of Bermuda and 13 km from the aircraft carrier USS Essex. The next crewed flight was Apollo 8, already to the Moon.

## What the Moon looked like from Earth on launch day

We calculated the phase at the moment of launch, October 11, 1968 at 15:02:45 UTC. From Earth the Moon was waning, 77% illuminated, on the 19th day after the new moon. The distance from the center of Earth to the center of the Moon was 405,100 km.
