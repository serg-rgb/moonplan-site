To land on the Moon, a lunar module was needed, and before Apollo 9 it had never been tested with people on board. This flight was meant to check it in every respect, not at the Moon but in Earth orbit.

## The crew and call signs

Commander James McDivitt, command module pilot David Scott and lunar module pilot Russell Schweickart. The two spacecraft had their own call signs: the command and service module was named Gumdrop, and the lunar module Spider.

[[figure:crew]]

## The launch

The Saturn V lifted off on March 3, 1969 at 11:00 a.m. US East Coast time, which is 16:00 UTC, from Launch Complex 39A. According to NASA, Apollo 9 was the third crewed flight of the program and the first to carry a lunar module.

[[figure:launch]]

## Flight timeline

| Date and time, UTC | Event |
|---|---|
| March 3, 16:00 | Launch from Complex 39A |
| March 5 | First TV broadcast, almost 7 minutes |
| March 6 | Schweickart's spacewalk, 37.5 minutes |
| March 7, 13:03 | The lunar module separates from the command module |
| March 7, 19:02 | Re-docking |
| March 13, 17:00:54 | Splashdown in the Atlantic Ocean |

The flight lasted 241 hours and 54 seconds, a little over ten days.

## The spacewalk

On March 6 Schweickart went out onto the platform by the lunar module hatch and spent 37.5 minutes outside. He was testing the spacesuit's portable life support system. For this excursion he had a call sign of his own, Red Rover, after the color of his red hair. At the same time Scott opened the side hatch of the command module and also worked outside.

[[figure:eva]]

## Undocking and rendezvous

On March 7 at 13:03 UTC Spider, with McDivitt and Schweickart, separated from Gumdrop. During the module's independent flight, its ascent stage engine was fired in space for the first time. At 19:02 UTC the spacecraft docked again.

[[figure:spider]]

[[figure:gumdrop]]

## The return

The command module splashed down on March 13, 1969 at 17:00:54 UTC in the Atlantic Ocean, about 180 miles east of the Bahamas, within sight of USS Guadalcanal. Gumdrop is now on display at the San Diego Air & Space Museum.

## What the Moon looked like from Earth on launch day

We calculated the phase at the moment of launch, March 3, 1969 at 16:00 UTC. The Moon was almost full, 99.6% illuminated: the full moon came 13 hours later, on March 4 at 05:18 UTC.
