Until 1873 Japan lived by a lunisolar calendar. Its months ran from new Moon to new Moon, and its years were kept in step with the Sun by inserted months. Today this calendar is called kyūreki (旧暦), the "old calendar". It has long been out of official use, but the festivals, the names of the months and the custom of admiring the autumn Moon have stayed.

## The last old calendar

The National Astronomical Observatory of Japan (NAOJ) points out that, strictly speaking, kyūreki means the calendar computed under the Tenpō system (天保暦). It was compiled by the astronomer Shibukawa Kagesuke and was in force for 29 years, from 1844 to 1872. Before it came the Kansei calendar, and after it the Gregorian one.

The change came suddenly. The decree replacing the calendar was issued on the 9th day of the 11th month of the 5th year of Meiji, when calendars for the following year were already on sale. And the 3rd day of the 12th month of that year was declared 1 January of the 6th year of Meiji, that is 1873. In Japan, 3 December is still called Calendar Day. Old dates went on being printed next to the new ones for a long time, up to the calendar for the 42nd year of Meiji.

## How a month was built

Each month began on the day of the new Moon, which was its first day. The mean length of a lunar month in the Tenpō system is about 29.53 days, so months had 29 or 30 days, and twelve of them fall short of the solar year by roughly 11 days.

To keep the calendar from drifting away from the seasons, a thirteenth month was inserted. For this they used solar markers: the twenty-four seasonal divisions, nijūshi sekki (二十四節気). Twelve of them are chūki (中気), and these include the equinoxes and the solstices. NAOJ describes the rule like this: a lunar month containing no chūki becomes the inserted one and takes the name of the previous month with the prefix "uru" (閏). An example is "inserted ninth", uru-kugatsu, as in 2014. Over 19 years seven such months accumulate.

## Old names of the months

Each month also had a Japanese name. NAOJ warns that the origin of these names is interpreted in different ways, and that they differ from the months of today's calendar by about a month in season, on average.

## Rokuyō: six days in a circle

Japanese wall calendars still print rokuyō (六曜), a cycle of six days: senshō, tomobiki, senbu, butsumetsu, taian, shakkō. According to the National Diet Library of Japan, rokuyō came from China around the 14th century and became widespread only at the end of the Edo period.

The cycle is tied to the old calendar. The first day of the 1st and 7th months always begins with senshō, the first day of the 2nd and 8th with tomobiki, and so on in order. Within a month the days simply go round. So when you look at a modern calendar, rokuyō now and then "jump": that means a new lunar month has begun. Each day in Japan is given its own meaning, but the library notes that interpretations differ and there is no single correct one.

## Tsukimi: admiring the autumn Moon

Japan's best-known Moon festival is the viewing of the Moon on the 15th day of the 8th month of the old calendar. This Moon is called chūshū no meigetsu (中秋の名月), "the beautiful Moon of mid-autumn", or simply jūgoya (十五夜), "the fifteenth night". According to NAOJ, the custom came from China around the 9th century.

The date is worked out like this: it is the 15th day of the lunar month that contains the autumn equinox. On the Gregorian calendar it falls between 7 September and 8 October.

Interestingly, the "fifteenth night" is by no means necessarily a full Moon. The month begins on the day of the new Moon, and the 15th is the day when the Moon's age reaches 14 days. Half a lunar month, meanwhile, is about 14.8 days. So, as NAOJ writes, the full Moon more often comes after the festival.

A month later, on the 13th day of the 9th month, people admire another Moon, jūsan'ya (十三夜). NAOJ writes that this custom is purely Japanese: according to tradition it began with a Moon-viewing party held by the retired Emperor Uda in 919. The thirteenth night is never a full Moon.
