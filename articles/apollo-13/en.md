Apollo 13 was meant to be the third Moon landing. An oxygen tank exploded in the service module 55 hours and 54 minutes after launch. The landing was cancelled, and the next four days were spent getting the crew home. They succeeded: on April 17, 1970, all three splashed down in the Pacific Ocean.

## The crew

Commander James Lovell, command module pilot John Swigert and lunar module pilot Fred Haise. The command module was called Odyssey and the lunar module Aquarius.

[[figure:crew]]

## Launch

The Saturn V lifted off on April 11, 1970 at 19:13 UTC. The first trouble came during ascent: the centre engine of the second stage shut down 132 seconds early. The other four engines burned 34 seconds longer, the third stage made up the missing speed, and the spacecraft entered the planned trajectory to the Moon.

[[figure:launch]]

## What exploded

On April 14 at 02:24 UTC the crew held a television broadcast. Soon after it ended, at 03:06:18, Swigert switched on the fans that stir the oxygen in the service module's tanks. According to the findings of the review board, the wires inside tank No. 2 had been damaged back during pre-flight testing. They short-circuited, the Teflon insulation caught fire, the pressure in the tank rose, and at 03:07:53 UTC the tank exploded. The explosion damaged the neighbouring tank No. 1 and the interior of the service module and blew off the bay panel.

Without oxygen the command module could no longer supply the crew with air and electricity. The astronauts moved into the lunar module, and the command module was shut down.

## The way home

The lunar module was designed for two people. Now it had to bring three back to Earth. At 08:43 UTC the crew fired its descent engine and put the spacecraft on a free-return trajectory: a flyby of the Moon and a return to Earth without additional manoeuvres.

[[figure:farside]]

After the flyby, on April 15 at 02:40:39 UTC, the descent engine was fired again for 263.4 seconds. This sped up the return: splashdown was moved from the Indian Ocean to the Pacific and brought forward by about nine hours. To save electricity and air, everything in the lunar module was switched off except life support, communications and telemetry.

Carbon dioxide became a separate problem. The supply of lithium hydroxide for scrubbing the air in the lunar module was limited, and the canisters from the command module did not connect directly to its system. On Earth an adapter was devised from improvised materials, tested and proposed to the crew. The result was the "mailbox".

[[figure:mailbox]]

The flight lasted 142 hours 54 minutes 41 seconds.

## The service module, seen first-hand

The service module was kept docked almost to the end so that it would shield the command module's heat shield. On April 17 at 13:15 UTC it was separated, and the crew saw and photographed the damage for the first time.

[[figure:damage]]

The lunar module was separated about an hour and a half before splashdown. Whatever was left of it after reentry fell into the Pacific Ocean northeast of New Zealand. That included the SNAP-27 generator with 3.9 kg of plutonium, which was meant to power the scientific instruments on the Moon.

## What the Moon looked like from Earth at the time of the accident

We calculated the phase for the moment of the explosion, April 14, 1970 at 03:07:53 UTC. From Earth the Moon was waxing and 55% lit, slightly past first quarter: 7.96 days had passed since the new moon on April 6. The distance from the centre of the Earth to the centre of the Moon was 403,629 km.

## The return

The Odyssey command module splashed down on April 17 at 18:07:41 UTC southeast of American Samoa, 6.5 km from the USS Iwo Jima, which picked up the crew.
