The Moon has no light of its own. What we see is sunlight bouncing off its surface, and the Sun always lights exactly half of the lunar globe. The only thing that changes is how much of that lit half faces us. That is all a phase is.

## What it looks like from outside

Picture a lamp in the corner of a dark room and a ball held at arm's length. Turn slowly on the spot and you will see the whole cycle. The ball is dark when it sits between you and the lamp. Then comes a thin crescent, a half, an almost full circle, and a full one when the lamp is behind you. After that it all runs backwards.

The Moon works the same way. When it is between Earth and the Sun, its night side faces us: that is the new Moon. A week later it has moved a quarter of the way round, and we see exactly half the disc lit. A week after that it is on the far side of Earth and its whole day side faces us: full Moon.

[[figure:phases-orbit]]

The eight phases people usually name:

| Phase | What you see | When the Moon is up |
|---|---|---|
| New Moon | nothing | in daytime, with the Sun |
| Waxing crescent | a thin crescent on the right | in the evening, low in the west |
| First quarter | the right half of the disc | from about noon to midnight |
| Waxing gibbous | more than half | from evening until nearly dawn |
| Full Moon | the whole disc | all night, rising at sunset |
| Waning gibbous | more than half, lit from the left | from late evening to morning |
| Last quarter | the left half of the disc | from about midnight to noon |
| Waning crescent | a thin crescent on the left | before dawn, in the east |

That is the sky as seen from the Northern Hemisphere. In the Southern Hemisphere everything is mirrored, so a waxing Moon is lit on the left. Near the equator the crescent lies almost flat, like a boat.

## It is not Earth's shadow

A common mistake is to think the dark part of the Moon is Earth's shadow. Earth's shadow only falls on the Moon during a lunar eclipse, and only at full Moon. On ordinary days the dark part is simply not lit by the Sun. It is lunar night.

Sometimes you can see that night side. When the crescent is very thin, the rest of the disc glows a faint grey. This is earthshine: sunlight reflected by Earth reaches the Moon and bounces back to us. Leonardo da Vinci wrote down the explanation in the early 1500s.

## How long a lunar month lasts

One full round of phases, from new Moon to new Moon, is the synodic month. NASA gives its average length as 29.53 days. The Moon actually goes round Earth faster, in 27.32 days. That is the sidereal month, measured against the stars.

The two days or so of difference come from Earth's own motion. While the Moon completes one orbit, Earth moves about 27 degrees along its path around the Sun. To get back between Earth and the Sun, the Moon has to make up that angle, and that takes roughly two more days.

29.53 days is an average. Real lunar months differ noticeably from one another, because the Moon's orbit is an ellipse and the Moon does not move along it at a steady speed. We calculated all 1,236 lunar months from 2000 to 2099:

- the shortest starts on 16 June 2053 and lasts 29.27 days;
- the longest started on 18 December 2017 and lasted 29.82 days;
- the gap between them is 13.2 hours.

So you cannot predict the next new Moon by adding 29.5 days to the last one: the error grows to several hours within a year. Exact phase times come from a theory of the Moon's motion. Moonplan does this on your device, without an internet connection.

## Why the Moon rises later every day

As the Moon goes round, it shifts east across the sky by about 13 degrees a day. Earth has to turn a little further for the Moon to clear the horizon again, so moonrise comes about 50 minutes later each day on average. "On average" matters here. We calculated every moonrise in London for 2027: the mean delay is 50 minutes, but on some days it is only 11 minutes and on others 1 hour 35 minutes. The further you are from the equator, the wider that spread. Moonplan shows the exact moonrise and moonset for your city.
