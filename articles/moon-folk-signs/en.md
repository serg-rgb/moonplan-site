Everyone knows a few sayings about the Moon. A ring round it means bad weather, the young Moon is good for sowing, the full Moon brings frost. We took the oldest records we could find and set them against what is known today about the sky and the weather. Some of the signs held up, others did not, and with a few the truth turned out to lie somewhere else entirely.

## Where the signs come from

The best-known Russian collection is Vladimir Dal's «Proverbs of the Russian People», published in 1862. It holds several dozen sayings about the Moon. Most of them are in the section «Земледелие» (Farming), under weather and harvest, with a few more in «Суеверия — Приметы» (Superstitions and Signs). Dal wrote them down as he heard them, so they sometimes contradict each other: one section puts "Sowing on the young Moon means a good harvest" next to "Sow at the new Moon and the worms will eat it".

## The ring round the Moon

Dal has: "A ring round the Moon means wind." Irish schoolchildren recorded for the National Folklore Collection: "When there is a ring round the moon it indicates rain". In «Weather Lore» (1893) Richard Inwards gives the English "Far burr, near rain": the wider the ring, the nearer the rain. He also has an Italian variant: "Circle near, water far; circle far, water near".

Here the folk eye did not fail. The UK's Met Office explains that such a ring, a halo, appears when moonlight passes through tiny ice crystals in high cirrus and cirrostratus cloud. The most common halo has a radius of 22 degrees, roughly what a spread hand covers at arm's length. And cirrus often runs ahead of a weather front. The Met Office says outright that halos were used for judging the weather for this reason. It is no promise of rain, though: high cloud can pass without any precipitation.

A wide ring is a halo, ice in the clouds. A small coloured rim close against the Moon is something else, a corona. It comes from water droplets, and the smaller the droplets, the larger the corona. The saying "circle far, water near" seems to tell them apart: the distant circle is the halo, the one that shows up ahead of a front.

## The horns of the crescent

"Steep horns of the month mean fair weather; flat ones mean foul," Dal wrote. And also: "A clear, steep-horned Moon means hard cold in winter and fine weather in summer."

The tilt of the crescent does not depend on the weather. It is set by the angle at which the Moon's path across the sky meets the horizon, and that angle changes with the seasons and repeats every year. But the sign has an honest part too: the word "clear". A sharp, bright crescent is seen in dry, transparent air, and a dry clear winter night really is cold. A dim crescent in a haze points to moist air and cloud on the way. Dal recorded that as well: "a dim month means wet; a clear one means dry".

## A red Moon at the horizon

"Red means wind," says the same long entry. NASA explains the colour of a low Moon simply: near the horizon its light reaches us through a much thicker layer of air, blue rays are scattered more, and the Moon turns yellow or orange. That is a property of the air column, not a promise of wind.

The size is even more interesting. A low Moon looks enormous, yet in photographs its diameter at the horizon and high in the sky is the same. NASA calls this the Moon illusion and admits there is still no exact explanation for it. Astronauts in orbit see the same illusion, although they have no trees or houses to compare it with.

## Full Moon and frost

Among gardeners this is perhaps the most persistent sign. The meteorologist Alistair Fraser of Penn State writes that he knows of no reason, and no data, that would make frost more likely at full Moon. The explanation most likely lies with the observer: people notice the full Moon on clear nights, and a clear, cloudless night cools fast. The clear sky brings the frost, and the Moon is simply easy to see against it.

## Sow, cut, salt

Dal has many farming signs, and they often argue with each other:

| Sign in Dal | Section |
|---|---|
| Sowing on the young Moon means a good harvest | Farming |
| Sow at the new Moon and the worms will eat it | Farming |
| In the first days of the new Moon, sow peas | Farming |
| Sow spring rye and wheat at the full Moon, oats two days after or before | Farming |
| Cut timber at the new Moon: wood felled on the waning Moon rots | Superstitions and Signs |
| Firewood, timber and brushwood cut at the full Moon will rot and the worms will bore it | Superstitions and Signs |
| Fish bite on the young Moon | Superstitions and Signs |

There is no scientific confirmation for these rules. We cover sowing by the Moon's phases in more detail in a separate article on the lunar calendar for the garden. But as part of a village calendar they make sense: the Moon can be seen from any field, and its phases were a convenient way to schedule work.

## So what is left

Of the Moon signs, the weather ones hold up best, and they hold up not because of the Moon but because of the clouds and the air through which we see it. A halo ring, a dim crescent and a clear cold Moon do tell us something about the weather. It is not the Moon itself, but the fact that it shows the state of the sky so well.
